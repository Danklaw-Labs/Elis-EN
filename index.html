The City-State of Elis

Olympic aficionados will recognize Elis as the city-state holding the Olympic Games in Olympia. Elis was a Greek city-state in the northwest of the Peloponnesian peninsula and was founded somewhere in the 5th century BC. Elis quickly became an important regional power, claiming hegemony over Olympia, the Pisatis in the south, and Lepreon in Triphylia further down the coast.

According to mythology, the ancient Eleans traced their ancestry to the mythological Dorian invasion and the legendary King Oxylus, under whom the first unification of Elis purportedly took place around the 11th century BC; however, the physical capital city of Elis wasn’t formally consolidated into a single urban center until around 471 BC. Initially, the Olympic Games, which were re-established in 776 BC, were administered by the neighboring city of Pisa. Unwilling to accept Pisan control over such a prestigious religious and cultural institution, Elis contested this authority. Backed by Spartan support, the Eleans successfully seized control of the sanctuary of Olympia around 576 BC, ultimately destroying the city of Pisa entirely by 572 BC.

Despite Elis being a staunch ally of the Spartans and joining the Spartan-led Peloponnesian League early, the relationship fractured during the Peloponnesian War as Sparta formally recognized Lepreon as independent. Outraged by Sparta’s recognition of the separatists, Elis switched allegiance and joined the Athenian-led Quadruple Alliance in response. Furthermore, Elis refused to let the Spartan King Agis join the sacrificial ceremony at the Olympics.

Eventually, the Spartan-led Peloponnesian League won the war and sought retribution against their former ally. Around 402–400 BC, Sparta issued an ultimatum to Elis, demanding that Elis grant total independence to its subject towns. When Elis refused, King Agis led successive invasions into Elean territory. Although the Eleans managed a strong military defense bolstered by 1,000 elite Aetolian mercenaries, exhaustion from the sustained Spartan devastation campaigns forced them to surrender. They were subsequently compelled to cede vast amounts of territory, ending Elis’s status as a regional power for good.

During the Macedonian invasion, Elis sided with the Macedonians but refused to fight other Greeks and renounced the alliance after the death of Alexander the Great. Elis joined the Aetolian League and frequently came into conflict with the Achaian League. However, around this time, a powerful non-Greek power started to intervene in their affairs: the Roman Republic. The Romans masterfully played into the existing divisions and initially joined the Aetolian League’s side before turning against them and conquering most of Greece, Elis included.

When Rome embraced Christianity during Late Antiquity, and in 391 AD as part of a wider campaign against paganism, Emperor Theodosius outlawed the Olympics under the pretext of it being a pagan tradition and dismantled Olympia’s sanctuary. Elis, as a province, suffered greatly afterward, and the Olympic Games would only return in 1896 as an international competition rather than a pan-Hellenic one.
